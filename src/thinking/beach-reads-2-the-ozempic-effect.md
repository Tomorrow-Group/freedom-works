Once upon a Wednesday, in a marketing war room lined with UX heatmaps and recycled buzzwords, a problem surfaced.

Nobody was hungry anymore.

Not metaphorically. *Literally*. Appetite was vanishing. Clicks were down. Cravings were muted. Fast food chains were offering loyalty points for just finishing a meal. Snack brands were quietly shrinking product lines. Influencer \#SponCon for protein shakes was tanking. And all the while, the drugs rolled in. Little pens with names like Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro - whispering sweet nothings to the hypothalamus.

Desire itself was under siege.

The CMO of a legacy indulgence brand, we’ll call her Madeline, stared at the Q2 reports and chewed a nail she hadn’t painted in weeks. *Consumers weren’t just eating less. They were feeling less.* Their attention spans were sharper, colder, more transactional. Less binge, more browse. Less lust, more list.

Madeline summoned the team. “This isn’t just about calories. This is about *wanting*.”

A strategist suggested repositioning the brand as a mindfulness tool. A copywriter drafted an email campaign headlined: *“Savour Slowly, Feel Fully.”*

Sales dropped another 3%.

In Miami, a frozen meal conglomerate pivoted. “GLP-1 Friendly” appeared on packaging - high protein, low sugar, zero joy.

In Brooklyn, a DTC startup launched “emotional snacking” - nostalgic flavours from childhood with cognitive prompts like *“You were safe once.”*

In L.A., a content strategist coined a new metric: Desire Per Pixel (DPP). She was hired immediately by TikTok.

Madeline, meanwhile, booked a trip to Sardinia. Not for trendspotting. For anchovies, and salt, and sun-warmed peaches. She came back changed.

The next campaign wasn’t about hunger. It was about remembrance.

Not “eat more.”

But: *Remember what it felt like to want something with your whole body.*

It worked.

Because even if the chemicals in our brains rewrite how much we need, they can’t erase how it felt - *to crave, to long, to need*.

That was the angle now: marketing as archaeology. Digging for feelings buried under dopamine drip-feeds and medicated metabolisms.

Brands would no longer win by stoking appetite. They’d win by evoking memory. *Craving as cultural artifact.*

And so the work changed.

Slogans became softer. Colours deepened. The pace slowed. Every campaign whispered: *“You used to want this. You still do, somewhere.”*

And slowly, sales stabilised.

Because even in a world of engineered satiety, there is always something that lives in the gut.

A memory.

A longing.

A taste.

And for marketers, that’s the new battlefield: not the stomach, but the soul.
